Furthermore, the dissolution medium remains clear with minimal fragment distribution, confirming that the release mechanism has transitioned to a very slow, steady-state diffusion phase where the rate of hydration is effectively balanced by the structural integrity of the gel barrier, preventing rapid erosion even after nearly 24 hours in a flow-through system.

### Literature-Supported Typical Mechanisms
Literature on hydroxypropylmethylcellulose (HPMC) matrix systems establishes that the viscosity grade and ratio of HPMC are critical determinants of gel layer strength and erosion rates. As noted in studies regarding monolithic HPMC tablets (e.g., Cao et al., *Journal of Controlled Release*, 2005), higher viscosity grades (such as K4M used in the Standard formulation) form more robust, cohesive gel barriers that resist erosion and sustain drug release primarily through diffusion for extended periods. Conversely, lower viscosity grades or specific excipient interactions can accelerate disintegration; for instance, Kajiyama et al. (*Chemical and Pharmaceutical Bulletin*, 2008) demonstrated that the addition of inorganic salts can disrupt gel formation and improve disintegration, implying that the absence of such disintegrants or the presence of high-viscosity polymers promotes the "static" behavior observed here. Typically, as the viscosity of the polymer increases, the gel layer becomes thicker and more resistant to mechanical stress from the flow-through medium, shifting the release kinetics from erosion-controlled to diffusion-controlled, which aligns with the lack of fragmentation and size reduction seen in the current observation.

This implies that the formulation design successfully prevented the "burst" or rapid terminal release often seen in weaker matrices, though it also raises the question of whether complete drug release was achieved within the 24-hour window or if a significant drug load remains trapped within the un-eroded core. *Note: This interpretation is a hypothesis based on visual morphology and general polymer physics; it assumes the internal drug concentration gradient is the sole limiting factor without accounting for potential pH-dependent solubility changes of methylene blue that might not be visually apparent.*
